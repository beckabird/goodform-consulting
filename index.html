The order of operations for opening a behavioral health program

Most launch delays come from doing the right things in the wrong order. Here is the sequence that keeps each step from undoing the last.

Adelle MutumManaging Operator, GoodForm Consulting
[Photo: Article cover image, 1600×900]

Founders rarely stall because they skipped a step. They stall because they did a step too early: signed a lease before checking zoning, filed for a license before policies existed, or hired a clinical director before the budget could carry payroll through the licensing wait. The fix is sequence.

1. Decide the level of care before anything else

Level of care drives everything downstream: the license type, the building requirements, staffing ratios, the policies you need and the payers you can contract with. Residential, partial hospitalization, intensive outpatient and outpatient each carry different rules in most states. Choose first, then plan.

2. Build the budget around the licensing wait

A program usually carries rent, insurance and some payroll for months before the first admission. Build a startup budget and pro forma that cover that runway. Many state applications also ask for evidence of financial viability, so this document does double duty.

3. Form the entity and settle ownership

Licenses, leases, bank accounts and payer contracts all attach to the entity. Ownership disclosures appear on most license applications. Settle the operating agreement before you sign anything else in the company's name.

4. Secure the site, after zoning and occupancy checks

Confirm the property is zoned for your use and that the building can meet occupancy and life-safety requirements for your level of care. Then negotiate the lease, ideally with contingencies tied to approvals.

5. Write policies before you file

Most license applications expect a policy and procedure manual that matches your services. Writing it first also forces decisions about intake, clinical workflow, privacy under HIPAA and 42 CFR Part 2, and incident handling that you would otherwise make in a rush.

6. File for licensure, then staff to the plan

With entity, site, budget and policies in place, the application is complete on the first submission. Staff in the order your state requires, with credentials verified and files built to survey standard from day one.

7. Prepare for accreditation and launch

Accreditation readiness starts the day you open. Run a mock survey early, close the gaps, and launch marketing and referral development once you can admit.

The short version: level of care, budget, entity, site, policies, license, staff, survey readiness, launch. Each step makes the next one faster.

What a Joint Commission mock survey actually tests

A good mock survey is not a checklist review. It follows patients and staff through your program the way surveyors do.

Adelle MutumManaging Operator, GoodForm Consulting
[Photo: Article cover image, 1600×900]

The Joint Commission evaluates organizations largely through tracer methodology. Surveyors pick a patient record or a process and follow it through the program, talking to the staff involved and checking that what happens on the floor matches what your policies say. A useful mock survey does the same.

Individual tracers

A surveyor selects a patient and follows their care from intake through assessment, treatment planning, services and discharge. They read the record, then ask the staff who touched it to explain what they did and why. Gaps usually show up where documentation and practice disagree.

System tracers

System tracers follow a process across the whole organization, such as medication management, infection control or data use. They test whether a policy is understood by everyone it applies to, not only by the person who wrote it.

Document and environment review

Expect review of policies, personnel files, credentials, training records, emergency management plans and the physical environment. Personnel files are a common weak point in new programs: missing competencies, expired credentials and incomplete orientation records.

Staff interviews

Surveyors talk to frontline staff, not only leadership. Staff should be able to describe how they report an incident, protect patient privacy and escalate a safety concern in their own words.

What you get from a mock survey

  • A findings list tied to the relevant standards
  • A prioritized corrective action plan with owners and dates
  • Staff who have already been through the interview experience once
Timing matters: run the mock survey early enough to fix what it finds. A findings report two weeks before the real visit gives you a list of problems, not a plan.

Real estateOctober 20265 min read

Check zoning and occupancy before you sign the lease

The most expensive launch mistake is a signed lease on a building that cannot be licensed for your use.

Adelle MutumManaging Operator, GoodForm Consulting
[Photo: Article cover image, 1600×900]

A building can look perfect, sit in the right market and come at the right price, and still be impossible to license for behavioral health. The lease does not care. Here is what to confirm before you sign.

Zoning for your specific use

Zoning codes often treat residential treatment, outpatient clinics and recovery housing differently. Some uses are permitted outright, some need a conditional use permit and a public hearing, and some are not allowed at all. Ask the local planning office how they classify your exact use, and get the answer in writing where you can.

Occupancy and life safety

Licensing agencies and fire marshals look at occupancy classification, egress, fire suppression, accessibility and bed counts. A building that works as an office can need significant buildout to serve as a residential program. Price that work before you commit.

Licensing agency requirements

Many states set their own physical plant rules for licensed programs: room sizes, bathroom ratios, kitchen standards, outdoor space and more. These sit on top of local building codes.

Protect yourself in the lease

  • Contingencies tied to zoning approval and licensure
  • A due diligence period long enough to get real answers
  • Clear responsibility for buildout costs
  • A use clause that matches your licensed services
Rule of thumb: if you cannot get a confident answer on zoning and occupancy, you are not ready to sign.

AccountingOctober 20266 min read

What a startup pro forma for a treatment program should include

Lenders, investors and many licensing agencies want to see the numbers. A credible pro forma answers the questions they will ask before they ask them.

Adelle MutumManaging Operator, GoodForm Consulting
[Photo: Article cover image, 1600×900]

A pro forma is your financial plan for the first years of the program: what it costs to open, how fast census grows, and when cash flow turns positive. Done well, it is also the document that keeps your launch decisions honest.

Startup costs

List everything you spend before the first admission: deposits and rent during buildout, furniture and equipment, EHR and phone systems, insurance, licensing and accreditation fees, legal and accounting work, and pre-opening payroll for the staff your state requires on day one.

A realistic census ramp

Few programs open full. Model census month by month, starting low and building as referral relationships and payer contracts mature. An optimistic ramp is the most common reason a pro forma fails its first review.

Revenue by payer

Break revenue out by payer type: commercial insurance, Medicaid, private pay and any contracts or grants. Each has different rates, authorization rules and payment timing. Include expected denials and write-offs instead of assuming every claim pays.

The credentialing gap

Payer credentialing and contracting take time, and claims take time to pay after that. Your model needs working capital to cover the months between opening and steady collections.

Staffing that matches your license

Build payroll from the staffing ratios and roles your level of care requires, not from the team you hope to have. Include benefits, payroll taxes, overtime and coverage for training time.

Scenarios

  • A base case you believe
  • A slower case with a delayed census ramp
  • A stress case with a payer delay or a vacancy in a required role
The test: if the slower case runs out of cash before break-even, you need more runway or a smaller opening plan. Better to learn that now than in month five.

HIPAA and 42 CFR Part 2: what new operators need to know

If you treat substance use disorders, HIPAA may not be the only privacy rule that applies to you. Here is how the two fit together.

Adelle MutumManaging Operator, GoodForm Consulting
[Photo: Article cover image, 1600×900]

Most healthcare operators know HIPAA. Fewer realize that substance use disorder programs can also fall under 42 CFR Part 2, a federal regulation that adds stronger protections for patient records. Knowing which rules apply shapes your consent forms, your EHR setup and your staff training.

Who Part 2 applies to

Part 2 generally applies to federally assisted programs that hold themselves out as providing substance use disorder diagnosis, treatment or referral. Federal assistance is defined broadly and can include things like Medicare or Medicaid participation, DEA registration or tax-exempt status. Confirm your status with counsel early.

How Part 2 differs from HIPAA

HIPAA allows many disclosures for treatment, payment and operations without specific patient consent. Part 2 has historically required written patient consent for most disclosures and limits how records can be used against patients, including in legal proceedings. Recent federal rule changes have brought Part 2 closer to HIPAA, but meaningful differences remain.

What it means in practice

  • Consent forms that meet Part 2 requirements, reviewed by counsel
  • Notices on disclosed records restricting further disclosure
  • EHR settings that can segment or flag protected records
  • Breach response procedures that cover both rules
  • Staff training that explains the difference in plain terms

Where programs get caught

The common failures are ordinary: a staff member confirms to a caller that someone is a patient, records go to a referral partner without proper consent, or a generic HIPAA consent form is used where Part 2 applies.

Start here: decide early whether Part 2 applies to you, then build consents, EHR settings and training around that answer. Have your attorney review the final forms.

HROctober 20265 min read

Personnel files that hold up to a survey

Surveyors pull staff files early, and new programs often fail here first. A consistent file structure fixes most of it.

Adelle MutumManaging Operator, GoodForm Consulting
[Photo: Article cover image, 1600×900]

Personnel files are one of the first places surveyors and licensing reviewers look, and one of the most common sources of findings in new programs. The problem is rarely missing people. It is missing paper.

What every file should hold

  • A signed job description that matches the role the person actually performs
  • Verification of licenses and certifications, checked with the issuing source
  • Background checks required by your state and level of care
  • Signed confidentiality and policy acknowledgments
  • Orientation records showing what was covered and when
  • Initial competency assessments for the person's role
  • Health screenings your state requires for staff
  • Annual training records and performance evaluations

Build it before you hire

Create the file checklist and folder structure before your first hire, so every file is built the same way from day one. Retrofitting files months later is slower and leaves gaps.

Track what expires

Licenses, certifications and some trainings expire. Keep one tracking sheet with expiration dates and an owner who reviews it monthly. An expired credential on survey day is an avoidable finding.

Audit a sample every quarter

Pull a few files at random and check them against the checklist. Fix gaps as you find them, and note the audit itself. It shows surveyors the process works.

Quick test: pick any file and ask whether a stranger could confirm, from the file alone, that this person is qualified, trained and cleared for their role. If not, the file is not done.
